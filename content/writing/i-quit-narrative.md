# I quit

*High-school personal narrative about moving from China to the U.S., national anthems, allegiance, and refusing to choose only one identity.*

Perhaps you’ve never heard of the chinese national anthem before. With a passionate swing of the voice, each note screams the pride of its people, the vibrance of its youth, the virtue of its soldiers, “Arise! Arise! Arise!” That was the sound of a song sung in unity, a sound, almost like an alarm, that went off every Monday morning when the school gathered to watch the flag rise.

I was born in a small city in the People’s Republic of China, a city with beauty but no fame, culture but no uniqueness, a vessel drained of its former glory. Growing up, I never understood what it meant exactly to be chinese. I have never met someone who was not, and frankly, I would quite easily mistake Africa for a country, if only I knew where that was on a map. But fate has always been the greatest of magicians, and fast forward a few years, I stepped onto a land foreign to me all but in name. The United States of America.

I was in fourth grade when I was introduced to this foreign world with foreign ideals, from singing “March of the Volunteers'', I had to do the pledge of allegiance, had to sing the “Star Spangled Banner'', and had to introduce to a school of mostly causasian students what life was like in a foreign country. My country. Yet I was never more proud of being “different” and of being able to identify as Chinese. Like a black pen against a white background, I have never felt so visible and so glad of my differences for making me seen. Where are you from? I’m from China. And you can instantly see a face of curiosity and accomplishment of someone who finally succeeded to get a conversation started. Can you say something in chinese? Sure. Can you teach me something? No problem. There was always a sentence starter for an awkward and boring conversation.
“Bang!” was the sound of an intense discussion bordering the edge of an argument. It’s hands flailing in the air, words echoing down the hall; it's walking back and forth, pointlessly, until you make some crucial argument. It’s dinner time, but I was the only one sitting, the others were either participating in the discussion or are intently listening to it. My mind starts to drift away.

Perhaps it was middle school social studies, or it was the presidential election of 2016. I began to recall the gradual isolation of a kid whose education no longer aligned with her friends’ in China, whose identity began to waver, and whose combination of knowledge became ever more confusing to her. I recall that year, the trade war between China and the U.S., when the first dose of confusion rushed down her as she was fed different knowledge from her teachers and her parents. I recall the year when she became an American citizen, and for the first time in her life, she lost sight of the kid standing and singing, “Arise! Arise! Arise!”

“I don’t know if I am more American or more Chinese”, she complained, out of frustration. “Both,” said her chinese american friend who shared her identity, but not her experiences.

The debate has gotten louder down the hall; my dad and grandpa have now made a complete lap around the house and are now starting round two. From Trump’s policies they have moved on to the 2020 election. From the treatment of asian americans they have moved on to international relations. No one, yet everyone, seems to have taken part of my mental turmoil.

The day in english class when her teacher asked the class to name the biggest villains in history. Up on the board, a list of names had already congregated- Hitler, Robespierre, Stalin… and Mao Zedong. It was strange to hear a name so familiar to you listed in an unfamiliar way. The founder of her country, the pride of her people, the face of the people’s republic of china. The villain. She felt conflicted having now knowledge she didn’t know what to do with. Maybe she shed a few tears, but more of bewilderment than in true sadness. She couldn’t help but confide in her mom the incident in class that day. Her mom looked at her, almost careless in reply, “This is politics. It's not a matter of people or events, it's a matter of how they are portrayed…”

I shrug a little. Some time during the argument a political debate had turned personal, and my always patriotic grandfather began to give a passionate speech of his values and his beliefs. Each word screams the pride of his people, the vibrance of his youth, the virtue of his soldier friends whom he knows well. The last sentence that I had cared to listen to from that argument was this, and it was addressed to me specifically, “Even though you are American, I still love you, and I hope that you remember your roots.”

At that precise moment I recalled the chinese child singing her national anthem, the american child citing her pledge of allegiance, and the lost soul being perplexed by two identities ripping her apart and threatening to create a rift between her family. Somehow none of that no matter matters. Her and I are no longer the same person, or perhaps we have always been. As I truly left behind my confusion and frustration in the face of politics and picking a side, I gave myself a way out, which is to no longer care about politics.

Am I more American or Chinese? Both.
