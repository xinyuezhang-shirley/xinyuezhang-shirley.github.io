# Pieces

*High-school final project essay — a fragmentary personal narrative written alongside Virginia Woolf’s Mrs Dalloway, about identity, memory, and becoming.*

Introduction

August (ambiguous date), 2021

I wish I can describe to you exactly how beautiful my world is: every frustration and headache, every laughter, every sorrow. I wish I can tell you how it is in my life, and I wish that you’d understand, but I’m sure that you don’t. If only I can give you my eyes, my heart, my brain, my everything, then maybe a part of me will stay with you, and you’d only then be able to see a slice of what it takes to be me. The rest I’m still trying to figure out myself.

I was born on June 9th, 2004 at some time in the afternoon. In every past version of myself, something echoes a fate that can only be chosen by myself. In every book I have read, every character I became, I became a little bit more myself. Everything I read and write is a fragment of myself. Every decision that I made and shall ever make transcends into me, and it is the thought of this that grants me comfort- the thought that I am every fragment of me. I invite you to understand me through the fragments of myself, though they are, just as I am, a piece of me, and though I will never truly know who I will be, what I want to be.

Chapter 1: Birth

My mother said I was an obnoxious toddler. I cried and cried, refused to eat what was fed to me, and rebelled against the world in the few ways my infancy allowed. I don’t remember my infancy, but I do remember that I cried often. Perhaps it ended when I first entered elementary school, or maybe the day when I first cried about a math test. My tears were never-ending, though those were the best years of my life.

I do not recall what life was like day to day before I moved to the U.S. All I recall now are the mundane, the little things that made me happy. I had a childhood friend who was three years older than I, we read stories together. She is now a sophomore in college, and I never had a friend as close as her. Decidedly, I chose to be lonely. In time, socializing grew daunting, and I wrapped myself in the cacoon. I fixated upon myself and my education. I forgot what life was like when I cried whenever I wanted.

“And there is a dignity in people; a solitude; even between husband and wife a gulf; and that one must respect, thought Clarissa, watching him open the door; for one would not part with it oneself, or take it, against his will, from one’s husband, without losing one’s independence, one’s self-respect—something, after all, priceless.”
— Virginia Woolf, Mrs. Dalloway (pg 120)

Mrs. Dalloway said she would buy the flowers herself.

Between each of her acquaintances and her was the distance of their thoughts, that drive them apart yet brings them relief. Each character in Dalloway lives in the world of their thoughts, as I live with mine. From Dalloway to Boxfish to Eyre, I feel the power of independence in their distance, from the world, from themselves, but not from their goals. The flowers were a symbol of her identity, and she needed to go alone, saying that she would be alone. As a teenager I tried to emulate the characters I read in books. I still do, decidedly, and never know what part of my character belongs to me. In the story of Dalloway, I saw a calmness that I do not have. Though I always liked to be alone, I dreaded loneliness equally. I was not Dalloway nor Boxfish or Eyre, but their stories inspire me to find the goals I would never stray away from. Dalloway’s phrases have a conclusiveness that comes with age, a decisiveness on what she wants and believes in, and that to me is the self-respect I wish to have in the turbulent world of my thoughts.

Hi, it’s me again. I am a sophomore now; fifteen. Fifteen was never perfect. Perhaps, life has never been perfect, alluding to the millions of quotes that I can easily cite for the imperfection of the world, both in the past, the present, or the future; your choice. If you might not remember, your fifteenth year of chaos began with perhaps the most chaotic birthday ever yet. There was no birthday cake or presents or friends…

I dislike many things. I am melancholic by nature and dissatisfied with many choices of mine, and perhaps that was why infant me cried on and on. I miss the summer still, but I often mean the summer that I spent at my grandparents’ house when I was six. I am very attached to my history that I often fear the future and how my family and my past self might dislike me in the present, might dislike me from the future. I could never put my thoughts down, never calm or controlled internally.

Decidedly, I dislike this project because I cannot find a way to make an argument about what I want for my future when I don’t have one. I do not stress about who I want to be, though this phrase is a partial lie. I worry about how the world perceives me, but I hide from the world. Should I change? I ask myself, often. Yet often I question my self-doubt, my motives for change, and my goals, and I never change. I write the same poems I wrote five years ago, though with more fluency in rhyme. My goal in life is to simply exist, though that is also a lie. I wish to be calmer and stop overthinking, I tell myself. Perhaps that should be my birthday wish this year. They never come true anyway.

Prayer
Whatever happens. Whatever
what is is is what
I want. Only that. But that.
—Galway Kinnell

In the words of Kinnell, “Whatever what is is is what I want”. This poem caught my eye the second I saw it. Short poems often do, for they seem to express an air of disinterest to impress the wide majority. Oftentimes I fear showing people my writing in case my words come across in ways I do not intend. For the past week, I have been trying to choose parts of my writing that are less fake, less pretentious, and less pessimistic. This poem reminded me that the more profound things often come in very simple lessons. In the three-lined poem of precisely fourteen words, the repetition is immaculate- four words contain “what”, along with three “is” in a row. Kinnell’s poem, though seemingly a very profound answer to what he wants in life, appears more like a rhetorical question, perhaps the reason for the title, “Prayer”. Though the message feels decisive, the diction choices invoke ambiguity, and “but that” refers to a change in thought immediately after the first thought. I identify with the thought of ambiguity and fear for knowledge. I fear that I’d become a cruel adult, an unloving partner, a despondent elder. I’d like to fit in, but fear who I might become. I have a fear of missing out, though often not of fun, but of all the different lives I could have lived by living in my head.

I will not return to a universe

of one great continent. The world
is flux, and light becomes what it touches,
becomes water, lilies on water,
above and below water,

and white and cerulean lamps,
small fists passing sunlight
so quickly to one another
that it would take long, streaming hair
inside my brush to catch it.
To paint the speed of light!
Our weighted shapes, these verticals,
burn to mix with air
and changes our bones, skin, clothes
to gases. Doctor,
if only you could see
how heaven pulls earth into its arms
and how infinitely the heart expands
to claim this world, blue vapor without end.
—Lisel Mueller

When I look at my life I sometimes refuse the concept of change, for fear nothing would be the same. Though I often wish to be more straightforward with my thoughts and stop the endless stream of anxiety, I fear that everything about this me would be unreal. In Lisel Mueller’s poem, when Monet faces the operation to remove cataracts, he speaks of all the ways that might alter his understanding of the world, all the sights that might disappear if his vision grew clearer. He spoke of painting “the speed of light… weighted shapes…”, the abstract, the unwhole, the personally valuable parts of his artistic identity. To him, if his vision was clear, if the lines were redrawn in his world, he loses the part of him who sees islands as “the lost children of one great continent”- the romantic and emotional vision. Using metaphors and symbolism to compare the real world to the wonderful sights he sees, Monet presents a fear of change in his artistic visions. Though I do not see great sights from islands, I too worry that I lose myself and the world I know if I stop worrying.

August (ambiguous date), 2021

Sometimes I wonder what death feels like, if death feels anything. To lie in peace with not thought and not feeling, to gradually ease out of existence painlessly, to have no sensation of pain or suffering. I imagine that it might be like slipping into deep coma, though rather than a dream, I’m met with a dark abysm of nothingness. Blind, deaf, senseless, meaningless. As a child I dreaded the concept of death, to lay in the darkness of a grave and bear an eternity of a state not proven to be inpermanent. And people will forget me, and I shall be erased from the face of the world…

[W]hen Evans was killed, just before the Armistice, in Italy, Septimus, far from showing any emotion of recognising that here was the end of a friendship, congratulated himself on feeling very little and very reasonably. The War had taught him. It was sublime.
— Virginia Woolf, Mrs. Dalloway (pg 86)

My great-grandmother died when I was seven years old. I was right by her side when it happened. The time between her last two breaths lasted eight seconds, and then she stopped breathing for all of eternity. I didn’t quite understand what death was when it happened, but now I realize that the sight is forever imprinted in my memories. Septimus Warren Smith didn’t understand death until he joined the war. He was a young poet who wanted to be noble until the war took away his agency and identity. Upon leaving, he didn’t feel sad, though not because he felt reasonably, but because the part of him that would be sad had died in the war. The irony in the war being “sublime” sticks only when we witness Septimus’s fits of madness. Rationally, we all know death the be the solution to all our problems this life, we tend to question our birth and the suffering that comes with our life. Though we all long for conclusiveness, we don't know what is on the other side of death. Most of us choose to not change because it is worthwhile, some of us choose to not change for fear of death. “To be or not to be?” Ultimately we don’t have a choice in what happens to us in life, if we ever needed one for anything.

Before I end I would like to apologize to whoever had to read this. It is unbearably long, yet this is the closest I can come to an argument about my life. When I was a toddler I wanted to be famous, when I was a pre-teen I wanted to be smart, when I was a teenager I wanted to fit in. I still want to be all of those things, but I mostly want to stop thinking about what I want now. I don’t really look forward to college, or dating someone, or getting a job, but I look forward to watching a movie next weekend, existing, and wearing my new shoes.

Perhaps that is enough. Only that. But that.
